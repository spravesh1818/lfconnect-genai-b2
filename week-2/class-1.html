Concept 01

Under the Hood, It's Next-Token Prediction

An essay, a poem, a line of code — everything an LLM writes gets built one small piece at a time, by asking the same question over and over.

  • At every step the model looks back over everything written so far and asks one question: what's the most likely next token?
  • It's not planning the whole answer in advance. It picks the next piece, tacks it on, then asks the same question again.
  • Your phone's autocomplete runs on this same basic skill. An LLM just does it with a much bigger model and a lot more text.
  • There's no separate "understanding module" tucked away in there. Fluent language just falls out of doing this one small thing, over and over.
# what the model does, one step at a time
prompt = "The capital of France is"
next_token = model.predict(prompt)
# next_token → " Paris"

prompt += next_token
# ...keep going until the model emits a stop token
Concept 02

From One Word to a Full Sentence

This "predict, append, repeat" cycle has a name: autoregressive generation. Each new token gets generated conditioned on every token that came before it.

  • Those faint curved lines? That's the model weighing how much each earlier word matters for the next prediction.
  • It keeps going until the model predicts a special end-of-sequence token, or it hits a length limit.
Concept 03

Training, at a High Level

That prediction skill doesn't come from nowhere — it's learned by nudging billions of internal numbers, called "parameters," until predictions start matching real text.

  • Self-supervised: the training text already has the answer baked in. The model hides the next word, guesses, then grades itself for free — no human labeling required.
  • Massive scale: we're talking trillions of words, pulled from books, websites, code, and articles — more than any one person could read in a lifetime.
  • Gradual adjustment: each wrong guess nudges the weights just a little. Do that billions of times and the predictions start sounding fluent.
  • Frozen snapshot: training eventually stops, which leaves the model with a knowledge "cutoff" date. Anything after that, it simply hasn't seen.
Concept 03, continued

Three Stages Turn a Predictor into a Helpful Assistant

On its own, raw next-token prediction just completes text plausibly. It won't follow instructions politely by itself — that takes two more stages.

STAGE 1

Pretraining

The model soaks up general language patterns, facts, and reasoning shapes just by predicting text across the open web.

STAGE 2

Supervised Fine-Tuning

Curated example conversations teach it what good instruction-following looks like, so it picks up the assistant "shape."

STAGE 3

Human Feedback (RLHF)

People rank several model responses side by side, and the model is tuned to favor the answers rated more helpful and safe.

Concept 04

The Transformer: a Quick Intuition

Modern LLMs are built out of transformer blocks, and their signature move is a mechanism called self-attention.

  • Self-attention means every word in a sentence can directly "look at" every other word to figure out what actually matters.
  • Older architectures like RNNs read text strictly left to right, one word at a time. That's slow, and they tend to forget words from way earlier.
  • Transformers instead process the whole sequence at once, which trains dramatically faster on modern hardware.
  • Stack dozens of these attention layers and the model builds up increasingly abstract representations of what things mean.

Concept 04, continued

What Attention Actually Buys You: Resolving Ambiguity

Attention is how the model works out which meaning of an ambiguous word fits, using clues from elsewhere in the sentence.

"I deposited cash at the bank."

Attention leans heavily on "deposited" and "cash" here, so the model reads bank as a financial institution.

"We sat by the river bank."

This time "river" pulls the weight, and here the same word bank means the edge of a river instead.

  • It's the same mechanism behind long-range dependencies: a pronoun on line 40 can still "attend to" a name introduced on line 1.

A Toy Example: Predicting the Next Word

In the companion notebook, we build a tiny from-scratch version of next-token prediction using nothing but word-frequency counts. No API key needed.

# a tiny toy model: just count what follows each word
corpus = "the cat sat the cat ran the dog sat"
from collections import defaultdict, Counter
follows = defaultdict(Counter)
words = corpus.split()
for a, b in zip(words, words[1:]):
  follows[a][b] += 1
# follows["the"] → Counter({'cat': 2, 'dog': 1})
  • Take the word "the" — this toy model just counts how often each word follows it in the training text.
  • "Cat" shows up twice after "the," "dog" only once, so the model predicts "cat" as the most likely next word.
  • Swap the word counts for billions of learned parameters and you've basically got a real LLM — the underlying question doesn't change.
  • Go open the notebook, run it yourself, and try feeding it a different tiny corpus.
Common Pitfalls

What an LLM Is Not

LLM output sounds fluent and confident, which invites a few tempting but wrong mental models. Here's what to watch for.

"It understands like a human"

It's manipulating statistical patterns in language. It doesn't have beliefs, senses, or goals of its own.

"It's just a fancy search engine"

By default it isn't looking anything up while it runs — it's generating text from learned patterns, and those patterns can be wrong.

"Bigger always means smarter"

Data quality, fine-tuning, and how well the task fits the model often matter just as much as raw parameter count.

"It remembers our last conversation"

Each conversation starts fresh unless there's an external memory system bolted on — any "memory" you notice is just context being resent.
